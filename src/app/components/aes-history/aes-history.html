The Story of AES

From competition to global standard - the journey of modern encryption.

The AES Competition

By the mid-1990s, the Data Encryption Standard (DES) was showing its age. With a 56-bit key length, it was increasingly vulnerable to brute-force attacks. In 1997, NIST announced an open competition to select a new encryption standard that would be secure, efficient, and freely available worldwide.

The competition attracted submissions from cryptographers across the globe. Unlike DES, which was developed in secret by IBM and the NSA, the AES selection process was completely transparent. All candidate algorithms were published for public scrutiny and cryptanalysis.

Why Rijndael Won

Rijndael was selected over strong competitors like Serpent (which had a higher security margin) and Twofish (from the creator of Blowfish). The key factors in Rijndael's victory were:

  • Performance: Excellent speed in both software and hardware across all platforms
  • Flexibility: Variable block and key sizes (though AES fixed the block size at 128 bits)
  • Simplicity: Elegant mathematical structure that aids in security analysis
  • Low memory requirements: Suitable for constrained devices
  • Parallelization potential: Operations can be computed in parallel

Design Philosophy

Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen designed Rijndael with a clear philosophy: security through mathematical elegance. The algorithm's structure is based on well-understood mathematical concepts that can be formally analyzed.

Security Analysis

Over two decades of intense cryptanalysis, no practical attack against AES has been found. The best known theoretical attacks include:

Attack Complexity Practical?
Brute Force (AES-128) 2^128 No
Biclique (AES-128) 2^126.1 No
Related-Key (AES-256) 2^99.5 No
Side-Channel Varies Implementation-dependent

The biclique attack, the best known attack against full AES, reduces the effective key strength by about 2 bits. This is cryptographically insignificant - even AES-128 with an effective strength of 126.1 bits remains completely secure.
